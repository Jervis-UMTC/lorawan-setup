CHAPTER III 
FRAMEWORK AND METHODOLOGY
Chapter 3 presents the framework and methodology used to design, develop, and 
evaluate the proposed blockchain-enabled LoRaWAN agricultural monitoring  
prototype. It discusses the study’s conceptual framework, research design, prototype  
architecture and experimental setting, development and data collection procedures,  
security boundary and threat model, evaluation methods, data analysis, and ethical  
considerations. Together, these sections explain how the prototype will be implemented 
and systematically tested under normal, connectivity-interruption, and selected security-
attack conditions. 
3.1 Conceptual Framework 
The Input–Process–Output (IPO) model was used as the conceptual framework 
because the study focuses on the design, operation, and evaluation of a technological  
prototype. The inputs consist of agricultural sensor data, LoRaWAN network events,  
system loads, and normal and attack test conditions. These inputs are processed through 
LoRaWAN transmission, Raspberry Pi edge computing, ChirpStack, MQTT, Node-
RED, PostgreSQL, Hyperledger Fabric verification, and Grafana monitoring. The  
resulting outputs are evaluated in terms of system performance, security effectiveness, 
data integrity, traceability, and resilience. The IPO framework therefore provides a clear 
and logical representation of how the system receives data, processes and protects them, 
and produces measurable outcomes. 
Figure 1 shows the study framework using the Input–Process–Output model.  
In the first step, the inputs consist of agricultural sensor data, LoRaWAN network  
events, and predefined test conditions. The sensors generate environmental and soil  
measurements, while network events represent normal transmissions, connectivity  
interruptions, unauthorized requests, replay attempts, and altered data. Normal and

In the process stage, sensor readings are first transmitted through LoRaWAN  
because it provides long-range communication with low power consumption, making it 
suitable for agricultural areas. A Raspberry Pi functions as the edge gateway and runs  
ChirpStack, MQTT, Node-RED, and local processing services. ChirpStack manages  
LoRaWAN devices and packets, MQTT supports lightweight data exchange, and Node-
RED controls data flow, validation, alerts, and automation. PostgreSQL stores  
structured information such as devices, locations, users, and alerts. Grafana retrieves the 
stored readings and system metrics to provide real-time dashboards and monitoring.

The output stage represents the measurable results produced by the prototype.  
System performance is assessed through packet delivery, end-to-end latency,  
transaction success, and resource utilization. Security effectiveness is examined through 
the system’s ability to detect or reject unauthorized access, replayed data, altered  
records, and post-storage tampering. Data integrity is confirmed by comparing the hash 
of a stored reading with the corresponding hash recorded in Hyperledger Fabric, while 
traceability is evaluated through the availability of timestamps, device identities,  
transaction records, and audit histories. System resilience is also examined by  
determining  whether  local  data  collection,  storage,  monitoring,  and  later 
synchronization can continue during temporary Internet or network interruptions.
3.2 Methodology 
3.2.1 Research Design
This study used a design and development research approach supported 
by experimental prototype testing. This approach was appropriate because the  
study involved creating, integrating, and evaluating a working Hyperledger  
Fabric-based security prototype for LoRaWAN IoT data in a smart banana  
plantation. Similar studies developed and experimentally tested LoRa–
blockchain and agricultural IoT prototypes to assess system functionality, data  
verification, access control, and transaction performance [25], [26], [31]. In this 
study, the prototype was designed to collect sensor readings, transmit and  
process the data, store complete records in PostgreSQL, and record their hashes 
in Hyperledger Fabric for verification. After development, the system was tested 
to determine whether it could reliably complete the data flow, verify stored

53
records, detect post-storage changes, and process blockchain transactions with  
acceptable prototype-level performance. 
3.2.2  Prototype  Architecture  and  Experimental  Setting
The study was conducted in a banana plantation in Tagum City, Davao  
del Norte, Mindanao, Philippines. The experimental testbed used agricultural  
sensors to collect soil and environmental readings. Figure 2 shows the  
components of the WisBlock Agriculture Kit used to develop the sensor nodes. 
The kit includes soil-moisture probes, UV-light sensors, barometric-pressure  
sensors, ambient-light sensors, environmental sensors for temperature and  
humidity, and rain sensors. Its RAK4631 core module processes the readings  
and sends them through LoRaWAN, while the WisBlock baseboards connect  
and power the sensor modules. The kit also provides RS485, 4–20 mA, and  
SDI-12 interfaces, allowing other agricultural sensors to be added as the  
prototype expands. Although an optional cellular module is included, it was not 
used because the sensor nodes transmitted their readings through LoRaWAN to 
the study’s Raspberry Pi gateway. 
Figure 3 shows the Raspberry Pi–RAK5146 LoRaWAN edge gateway  
used in the prototype. The gateway was assembled by mounting the RAK5146  
concentrator on its Pi HAT and connecting the assembly to the Raspberry Pi 4, 
together with the LoRa and optional GPS antennas. Raspberry Pi OS Lite was  
installed on a microSD card, after which the system was updated and secured by 
configuring SSH, disabling root login, and enabling the firewall. Docker was  
then installed, and the Raspberry Pi interfaces required by the concentrator—
SPI, I²C, and the serial port—were enabled. The gateway EUI was generated  
from the Raspberry Pi network interface, while the LoRa gateway software was 
deployed through Docker and configured to forward uplink and downlink  
packets to the ChirpStack network server. Because the prototype was deployed

ChirpStack was installed locally on the Raspberry Pi through Docker

send the processed data to the storage and verification layers. Because these

operational database, where complete sensor readings, device information,

to PostgreSQL through a restricted read-only account to display sensor readings 
and system-performance indicators. Hyperledger Fabric was deployed through  
Docker as a permissioned network within the local testbed, and a local  
application interface generated a cryptographic hash for each stored sensor  
record and submitted the hash, device identity, timestamp, and essential  
metadata to the Fabric chaincode. All components communicated through a  
private Docker and local-area network using defined service names, credentials, 
and required ports, allowing the database, dashboard, and integrity-verification 
functions to remain locally available during temporary Internet interruptions.

3.2.3 Prototype Development and Data Collection Procedure
The prototype was developed in stages, beginning with the assembly and 
testing of the agricultural sensor nodes and Raspberry Pi–RAK5146 LoRaWAN 
gateway. The required software services were then installed, configured, and  
integrated,  including  ChirpStack,  MQTT,  Node-RED,  
PostgreSQL/TimescaleDB, Grafana, and Hyperledger Fabric. After the gateway 
and sensor devices were registered, pilot tests were conducted to verify the  
complete data flow from sensor transmission and edge processing to database  
storage, dashboard display, and blockchain hash recording. Once stable  
operation was confirmed, the prototype collected data under normal operating  
conditions, temporary connectivity interruptions, and selected security-test  
scenarios. System logs, sensor records, blockchain transactions, network  
measurements, and resource-usage data were retained for subsequent  
performance and security analysis. 
3.2.4 Normal-Operation Performance Test Procedure 
A normal-operation performance test will be conducted to establish the  
baseline performance of the prototype before the security and resilience tests are 
performed. During this test, only a registered agricultural sensor and authorized 
system components will be used; no invalid traffic, simulated attacks, Internet  
interruptions, or service failures will be introduced. The sensor will transmit one 
reading every 15 seconds for 30 minutes, producing approximately 120 expected 
transmissions per run, consistent with the baseline period used in the resilience 
experiment. The test will be repeated three times under the same configuration  
to obtain consistent baseline measurements.

whether the prototype accepts only registered devices and authorized  
system users while rejecting invalid credentials and prohibited actions.

the allow-or-deny test of Iftekhar et al. [41], and the unauthorized  
blockchain-access test of Homaei et al. [47]. Unlike these earlier studies, 
the proposed test will examine authentication and authorization at four  
points in the data flow: LoRaWAN device activation, MQTT messaging, 
PostgreSQL/TimescaleDB storage, and Hyperledger Fabric transactions.
The test will determine whether the prototype accepts registered 
devices and authorized identities while rejecting invalid credentials and 
prohibited actions. Testing will be conducted on an isolated network  
using synthetic sensor data. Logs from ChirpStack, Mosquitto MQTT,

LoRaWAN device-authentication procedure
Three sensor configurations will be prepared: one registered  
device with the correct DevEUI, JoinEUI and AppKey; one device using 
a registered DevEUI but an incorrect AppKey; and one device using an 
unregistered DevEUI. For each trial, the sensor will be restarted and  
instructed to perform an OTAA join. ChirpStack will be observed to  
determine whether the JoinRequest is received and whether a JoinAccept 
is generated. When the authorized device joins successfully, it will send 
one test reading to confirm that the data reaches MQTT. Each of the three 
conditions will be repeated 10 times. A 30-second interval will be  
provided between attempts to avoid overlapping join procedures.
MQTT authentication and authorization procedure
Two MQTT accounts will be configured. The authorized Node-
RED account will be permitted to access the required sensor topics,  
while a limited test account will be prohibited from publishing to  
protected topics. Anonymous access will be disabled. From a separate  
test laptop, the researcher will attempt an authorized publish, a  
connection using an incorrect password, and a publish using valid  
credentials but a prohibited topic. Each condition will be repeated 10  
times using mosquitto_pub. Mosquitto and Node-RED logs will be 
checked to confirm that denied messages did not enter the processing  
pipeline.

Hyperledger Fabric authorization procedure
Three Fabric identities will be prepared: an authorized writer, a  
valid user without writing permission, and an invalid or untrusted  
identity. The authorized writer will submit a synthetic sensor hash and  
metadata through the chaincode. The other two identities will attempt to 
invoke the same function. Each condition will be repeated 10 times.  
Before and after every unauthorized attempt, the corresponding  
blockchain record will be queried to confirm that the world state did not 
change.
Measures and Data Analysis
For every trial, the researcher will record the expected decision, 
actual decision, response time, system message or error, and whether the 
request caused any unauthorized message or blockchain state change.  
The assessment will use the following measures:
● Authorized success rate: percentage of valid requests correctly 
accepted.
● Unauthorized rejection rate: percentage of invalid or prohibited 
requests correctly rejected.
● False acceptance: an unauthorized request that is incorrectly accepted.
● False rejection: an authorized request that is incorrectly rejected.
● Correct-decision rate: percentage of all requests correctly classified.
● Response time: time required for the system to accept, reject, or 
commit the request.
● Unauthorized state change: whether a prohibited request created or 
modified a protected record.

Response time will be measured separately at each layer. For

3.2.6.2 Replay or Spoofing Test Procedure
A replay attack occurs when a previously accepted message is captured 
and transmitted again to make the system process the same data more than once. 
Spoofing occurs when an unauthorized device creates a message that appears to 
come from a legitimate sensor. Few related systems experimentally evaluated  
these threats. Homaei et al. [47] inserted replayed and fabricated sensor readings 
into a water-consumption dataset and used an LSTM Autoencoder and Isolation 
Forest to identify them before blockchain storage. However, their tests were  
conducted at the data-processing layer rather than through actual LoRaWAN  
packet transmission. A recent LoRaWAN study by Pourghasem et al. [56] also 
evaluated spoofing detection using device and physical-layer attributes such as 
RSSI, battery level, and altitude, but its evaluation was dataset-based rather than 
a live rogue-device attack. 
--- PAGE 18 ---
67
Following the controlled-injection approach of these studies, the  
proposed prototype will conduct two practical LoRaWAN-level tests: uplink-
frame replay and device impersonation through a forged uplink. Testing will be 
performed only within the isolated experimental network using the authorized  
sensor node and a separate programmable LoRa test node. Frame-counter  
validation will remain enabled in ChirpStack. Each test will include 10  
legitimate control transmissions and 10 attack transmissions, producing a total

LoRaWAN Replay Test
The registered agricultural sensor will first transmit a legitimate uplink  
containing a synthetic sensor reading. The raw LoRaWAN physical payload,  
device address, frame counter, frequency, data rate, and time of reception will  
be recorded from the gateway and ChirpStack frame logs. The legitimate packet 
must appear as an accepted uplink in ChirpStack and must reach MQTT and  
Node-RED.
The legitimate sensor will then transmit at least three additional uplinks 
so that its frame counter advances. Afterward, the previously captured physical 
payload containing the older frame counter will be retransmitted through the  
programmable LoRa test node. The test node will use the same AS923  
frequency, spreading factor, bandwidth, and coding rate as the captured  
transmission.
For a trial to be considered valid, the gateway must record that it received 
the retransmitted radio frame. If the gateway does not receive it, the trial will be 
repeated because this would represent a transmission failure rather than  
successful replay rejection. Once received, ChirpStack should identify that the  
frame counter has already been processed and prevent the frame from producing 
a device uplink event. ChirpStack documents that a previously seen uplink frame 
counter produces a retransmission error and is not forwarded as a new  
application uplink when frame-counter checking is enabled.
After every attempt, the researcher will verify that the replayed message 
did not appear in:
● the ChirpStack application uplink events;
● the MQTT sensor topic;
● Node-RED’s accepted-message flow;

● PostgreSQL/TimescaleDB; or
● the Hyperledger Fabric transaction records.
The procedure will be repeated using 10 newly captured legitimate  
packets and 10 corresponding replay attempts.
LoRaWAN Spoofing Test
The spoofing test will simulate a second device attempting to send data 
using the identity of the legitimate agricultural sensor. The registered sensor will 
first transmit 10 genuine uplinks as the control condition. Each transmission  
must be received by the gateway, authenticated by ChirpStack, forwarded to  
MQTT, and stored through the normal processing flow.
For the spoofing condition, the programmable LoRa test node will create 
an uplink containing the current device address of the legitimate sensor and a  
fabricated sensor value. However, the test node will not possess the legitimate  
sensor’s network session key. Therefore, the message integrity code generated  
by the test node will not match the legitimate device’s credentials.
The forged frame will be transmitted using a valid AS923 radio  
configuration so that it can be received by the RAK5146 gateway. LoRaWAN  
uses session keys and a message integrity code to authenticate radio  
transmissions; when the session key is incorrect, ChirpStack cannot validate the 
MIC. The gateway may therefore receive the radio signal, but ChirpStack should 
reject the message before it is published to the application.
Each spoofing trial will be checked at three points:
1. The gateway frame log must confirm that the radio frame was received.
2. ChirpStack must report an authentication or MIC error and must not 
create an accepted device uplink.
--- PAGE 21 ---
70
3. MQTT, PostgreSQL/TimescaleDB, and Hyperledger Fabric must 
contain no record originating from the forged packet.
Ten forged uplinks will be transmitted. A spoofing attempt that is not received  
by the gateway will not be counted and will be repeated.
Measures and Data Analysis
For every trial, the researcher will record the gateway reception result,  
ChirpStack decision, downstream propagation, processing time, and occurrence 
of an unauthorized database or blockchain record. The following measures will 
be reported:
● Replay-rejection rate: percentage of received replayed frames prevented from 
reaching the application.
● Spoofing-rejection rate: percentage of received forged frames rejected 
because they could not be authenticated.
● False-acceptance count: number of replayed or forged frames incorrectly 
accepted.
● False-rejection count: number of genuine frames incorrectly rejected.
● Unauthorized propagation rate: percentage of attack frames that reached 
MQTT, Node-RED, PostgreSQL/TimescaleDB, or Hyperledger Fabric.
● Processing time: time from gateway reception until ChirpStack acceptance or 
rejection, when matching timestamps are available.
LoRaWAN Analyzer will support the assessment by displaying frame-
counter sequences and transmission events, while ChirpStack, MQTT, database, 
and Fabric logs will provide evidence of whether the packet moved to later  
layers. ChirpStack’s gateway-level frame logging can include frames that fail  
MIC or frame-counter checks, making it possible to confirm that a packet was  
received by the gateway but rejected before application processing.

3.2.6.3 Data Integrity Test Procedure
Data integrity refers to maintaining the accuracy and unchanged  
condition of sensor data while they are processed and stored. Most reviewed  
studies implemented integrity mechanisms but did not conduct direct tampering 
experiments. Studies [39], [42], and [44] used IPFS content identifiers or  
SHA-256 hashes, while Honar Pajooh et al. [43] computed checksums for off-
chain IoT data and stored them in Hyperledger Fabric for later verification.  
Other systems relied on LoRaWAN message integrity codes or blockchain  
ledger immutability [40], [41], [46], [48]. Homaei et al. [47] conducted a more  
direct assessment by inserting altered error codes before blockchain recording  
and modifying blockchain files after storage. The abnormal records were  
examined before acceptance, while changes made to stored blockchain files  
were rejected because their hashes no longer agreed with the other validator  
records.  
The present study will adapt the controlled tampering approach of  
Homaei et al. [47] and the checksum-verification design of Honar Pajooh et al. 
[43]. Two tests will be conducted: application-layer data alteration and post-
storage database tampering. The first will determine whether a sensor record  
changed during Node-RED processing can be detected before it enters the  
operational database and blockchain. The second will determine whether a  
PostgreSQL/TimescaleDB record changed after its original hash has been  
committed to Hyperledger Fabric can be detected through hash comparison.
--- PAGE 23 ---
72
Testing will be performed on an isolated network using synthetic  
agricultural sensor readings. Each test will include 10 unchanged control records 
and 10 deliberately altered records, resulting in 40 total test attempts. Before  
testing, the PostgreSQL/TimescaleDB database will be backed up, timestamps  
will be synchronized, and logging will be enabled in Node-RED,  
PostgreSQL/TimescaleDB, and Hyperledger Fabric.
Preparation of the Integrity Record
For every sensor reading, Node-RED will create a canonical record  
containing the test identifier, device identifier, frame counter, sensor type,  
sensor value, measurement unit, and event timestamp. The fields will be placed 
in a fixed order, timestamps will use the same UTC format, and numerical values 
will use consistent decimal precision. This standardization is necessary because 
differences in field order, spacing, or numerical formatting can produce different 
hashes even when the actual information has not changed.
Node-RED will generate a SHA-256 hash from the canonical record.  
The complete sensor record will be stored in PostgreSQL/TimescaleDB, while  
its record identifier, SHA-256 hash, device identifier, and timestamp will be  
submitted to Hyperledger Fabric. The same canonicalization procedure will be  
used whenever a stored record is verified.

Application-Layer Data-Alteration Procedure
A registered sensor will transmit a synthetic reading through LoRaWAN 
and ChirpStack to MQTT. When Node-RED receives the message, it will create 
the canonical record and calculate its initial SHA-256 hash. The record will then 
pass through the normal validation and processing flow.
For the control condition, the sensor value will remain unchanged. Node-
RED will calculate the hash again immediately before database storage. The  
initial and final processing hashes should match, allowing the record to be stored 
in PostgreSQL/TimescaleDB and its hash to be submitted to Hyperledger Fabric.
For the alteration condition, a controlled test function in Node-RED will 
modify one sensor value after the initial hash has been generated. For example, 
a soil-moisture value of 35% may be changed to 45%. The device identity, frame 
counter, and timestamp will remain unchanged so that the test represents  
unauthorized alteration of the reading rather than the creation of a different  
record.

not be stored as a valid operational reading or submitted to Hyperledger Fabric 
as a valid sensor transaction. Ten unchanged records and ten altered records will 
be tested.
Post-Storage Database-Tampering Procedure
Ten valid sensor readings will first be processed, stored in  
PostgreSQL/TimescaleDB, and committed to Hyperledger Fabric using their  
original SHA-256 hashes. Each record will have a unique test identifier linking 
the database entry to its corresponding Fabric transaction.
For the control condition, the integrity-verification function will retrieve 
each unchanged database record, convert it into the defined canonical format,  
recompute its SHA-256 hash, and compare the result with the hash retrieved  
from Hyperledger Fabric. All ten unchanged records should produce matching  
hashes.
For the tampering condition, a temporary database account created only 
for the controlled security test will be used to change the sensor value of each  
record after its original hash has been committed to Fabric. The blockchain  
record will not be changed. Immediately after each modification, the integrity-
verification function will be executed.
The function will retrieve the modified PostgreSQL record, recompute  
its hash, and compare it with the original hash stored in Hyperledger Fabric.  
Because the sensor value has changed, the hashes should not match. The system 
should mark the record as altered, produce an integrity warning, and identify its 
record identifier, device identifier, verification time, database hash, and  
--- PAGE 26 ---

blockchain hash. After the experiment, the modified records will be restored  
from the backup.
Measures and Data Analysis
For every trial, the researcher will record the expected integrity  
condition, actual verification result, original hash, recomputed hash, verification 
time, and whether the altered record entered the valid database or blockchain  
flow. The following measures will be used:
● Integrity verification rate: percentage of unchanged records correctly 
confirmed as valid.
● Tamper-detection rate: percentage of altered records correctly identified 
through a hash mismatch.
● False-positive count: unchanged records incorrectly identified as altered.
● False-negative count: altered records incorrectly reported as valid.
● Unauthorized storage count: altered application-layer records stored as valid 
operational records.
● Verification time: time from the start of the hash comparison until the system 
reports a match or mismatch.
Frequencies and percentages will be used to report integrity decisions.  
Verification time will be summarized using the mean and standard deviation.  
Results will be reported separately for application-layer alteration and post-
storage tampering because the first test examines protection before valid storage, 
while the second examines verification after storage.
The security requirement will be zero false negatives and zero  
unauthorized storage. Every unchanged record should produce a matching hash, 
while every deliberately altered record should produce a hash mismatch. The  
test verifies whether data remain unchanged after the first hash is generated; it

3.2.6.4 Traceability Test Procedure
Traceability refers to the ability of the prototype to identify the origin  
and complete processing history of every sensor reading. A traceable record  
should show which device produced the reading, when it was transmitted and  
processed, where the complete record was stored, and which Hyperledger Fabric 
transaction contains its hash and metadata. Previous studies commonly  
demonstrated traceability through blockchain histories and record queries rather 
than through direct attack experiments. Keo et al. [42] recorded the movement  
and history of agricultural batches, Honar Pajooh et al. [43] stored information  
about data ownership, submission time, storage location, and related records,  
while Yang et al. [44] used unique codes to retrieve complete product histories. 
Other studies used Fabric historian records, blockchain audit logs, and  
transaction histories to trace sensor readings and system activities [46]–[48].  
However, most did not report a separate traceability success rate or evaluate  
whether every stored record could be completely and correctly reconstructed. 
The present study will evaluate traceability through two tests: an  
individual sensor-record trace test and a device-history reconstruction test. The 
first will determine whether one sensor reading can be followed from its  
LoRaWAN source to PostgreSQL/TimescaleDB and Hyperledger Fabric. The  
second will determine whether a sequence of readings from one device can be  
retrieved in the correct chronological order with complete links between the  
operational database and blockchain records.
Testing will be conducted on the isolated prototype network using  
synthetic agricultural sensor readings. The individual-record test will use 10  
--- PAGE 28 ---
77
readings, while the device-history reconstruction test will use 10 sequences  
containing five consecutive readings each. This will produce 20 traceability  
trials involving 60 sensor records. Logging will be enabled in ChirpStack,  
Mosquitto MQTT, Node-RED, PostgreSQL/TimescaleDB, and Hyperledger  
Fabric.
Preparation of Traceability Records
Node-RED will assign a unique trace_id to every accepted sensor  
reading. The trace identifier will remain attached to the record as it passes

identity, timestamp, and essential metadata will be recorded in Hyperledger 
Fabric. The common trace identifier will allow the database record to be linked 
directly to its blockchain transaction.

3.2.6.5 DoS or flooding Test Procedure
A Denial-of-Service (DoS) or flooding attack occurs when a large  
number of requests or messages are sent within a short period to consume  
processing power, memory, bandwidth, or other system resources. The attack  
attempts to slow down the system or prevent legitimate sensor readings from  
being processed. Among the reviewed studies, Hou et al. [40] conducted the  
clearest DoS-like experiment by generating traffic from authorized and  
unauthorized devices. Invalid packets were rejected after failing message-
integrity verification, while system throughput, CPU utilization, and network  
bandwidth were measured. Their results showed whether legitimate traffic could 
continue while invalid traffic increased. The present study will follow the same 
mixed-traffic approach but adapt it to the MQTT and application layers of the  
proposed prototype. Radio-frequency jamming will not be included because it  
requires specialized equipment and may interfere with other wireless users. 
Two controlled flooding tests will be conducted: an MQTT connection-
flooding test and an invalid application-message flooding test. Both tests will be 
performed on an isolated local network using a separate laptop as the traffic  
generator. A legitimate agricultural sensor will continue sending one reading  
every 15 seconds throughout each test. This will allow the researcher to  
determine whether valid sensor readings remain available while invalid traffic  
is being received.
Each test will include three conditions: normal traffic, moderate  
flooding, and high flooding. Every condition will run for five minutes and will  
be repeated three times. Therefore, the two tests will involve 18 experimental  
--- PAGE 33 ---
82
runs. A five-minute recovery period will be provided after every run to allow  
the system to clear pending requests and return to normal operation.

3.2.6.6 Resilience Test Procedure

essential functions during a disruption and return to normal operation without

or server-interruption experiment. Homaei et al. [47] conducted a practical  
resilience test by interrupting Internet connectivity for two hours per day over  
one week. Their gateway temporarily retained the sensor readings and submitted 
the pending information after connectivity was restored. They evaluated whether 
records were lost and whether the delayed transactions caused blockchain  
conflicts.

3.3 Ethical Consideration
--- PAGE 41 ---
90
This study involved the development and testing of an IoT and blockchain-based 
prototype and did not collect information from human participants. The sensor gathered 
only environmental readings, such as temperature and humidity. No personal, medical, 
financial, or identifying information was included in the system.
The prototype was operated only for research purposes. Field installation and  
testing were conducted with the knowledge and permission of the concerned plantation 
management. The equipment was placed and operated in a way that did not interfere  
with normal plantation activities, damage crops, or create safety risks for farm  
personnel.
All device identifiers, access keys, login credentials, server addresses, and  
blockchain identities were handled securely. Sensitive technical information was not  
publicly disclosed to prevent unauthorized access to the prototype. The collected sensor 
records were used only for system development, verification, and performance testing.
The tamper-detection test was performed in a controlled environment. Only  
selected test records stored in PostgreSQL were intentionally modified, and no actual  
plantation records used for operational decisions were affected. The test was limited to 
determining whether the prototype could identify post-storage changes through hash  
mismatches.
The results were reported honestly, including successful and failed  
transmissions, performance measurements, and the limitations of the prototype. Since  
this dissertation expands an earlier published paper, the original publication will be  
properly cited and acknowledged. Similarity in the system, data, and results will be  
disclosed to maintain academic integrity and avoid presenting previously published  
work as entirely new.
